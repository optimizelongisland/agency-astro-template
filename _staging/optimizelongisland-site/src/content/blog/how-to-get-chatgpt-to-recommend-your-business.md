Nobody can promise that ChatGPT will recommend your business. What you can do is make it easy for an AI assistant to find, read and trust you. Assistants that search the web, including ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Claude, build their answers from pages they can reach and from what the wider web says about a business. A local business is more likely to be named when:

1. **Its own site says plainly what it does.** Services, towns served and, where you publish them, prices, written as direct answers to the questions customers ask.
2. **Its listings agree with its site.** The Google Business Profile and other business listings show the same name, address, phone number and hours as the website.
3. **Other sites mention it.** Reviews, directories and local articles that name the business give an assistant something besides your own word.
4. **Its pages are current.** A page that is clearly kept up to date is easier to rely on than one that looks abandoned.

That is the whole idea. The rest of this post explains each part for Long Island business owners and how to check where you stand.

## How does ChatGPT decide which businesses to recommend?

ChatGPT and similar assistants can search the web and then write an answer from what they find. When someone asks "who is a good electrician in Huntington," the assistant does not look up a list of approved businesses. It pulls pages that seem to answer the question, reads them, and puts together a reply, usually with links to the pages it used. An assistant also sometimes answers from what it learned in training, without searching the web at all. That is one reason a change to your site may not show up in its answers.

We can't see inside how any of these systems weigh their sources, and the companies that run them change them often. Treat anyone who quotes you an exact formula with suspicion. What is documented and easy to observe is simpler. The assistants can only use pages they can reach. They tend to quote pages that state facts clearly. And they look at more than your own site, so what other sites say about you matters too.

That points to three jobs: make your own pages clear, make your business details consistent everywhere, and earn mentions on sites assistants already read. The next sections cover each.

## What should your website say?

Your website should state the facts a customer would ask about, in plain sentences, near the top of the page. If an assistant has to guess what you do, it will use a competitor that spelled it out.

Here is a short list of what a local service business should have on its pages:

- **What you do.** One page for each main service, named the way customers search for it.
- **Where you do it.** The towns and counties you serve, such as Suffolk County or Nassau County, written out in words and not only in a map graphic.
- **What it costs, if you can say.** A starting price or a typical range gives an assistant something concrete to quote.
- **Answers to real questions.** A short FAQ with direct answers, 40 to 70 words each. Put the answer in the first sentence.
- **Who you are.** The business name, phone number and address or service area, the same on every page.
- **Structured data.** Hidden markup that tells search systems in a standard format what the business is, where it works and what the FAQ says. It does not replace good writing, but it removes guesswork.

## Why do your listings and reviews matter?

Listings and reviews matter because an assistant looks for agreement. If your website says you are in West Islip and open until 6, and your Google Business Profile says something else, the assistant has a reason to doubt both.

Start with the basics:

1. **Claim and complete your Google Business Profile.** Add your categories, service area, hours, phone number, website and a plain description. Our [Google Business Profile checklist](/blog/google-business-profile-optimization/) walks through it.
2. **Make your name, address and phone number match everywhere.** Use the same spelling and the same format on your site, your profile and every directory. Small differences, such as "Street" in one place and "St." in another, are worth fixing.
3. **Ask real customers for reviews.** Reviews on Google and on the directories your trade uses are among the mentions that help a business get noticed. Only real customers, only real experiences. Invented reviews break the platforms' rules and can get a listing removed.
4. **Remove or merge duplicates.** An old listing with a former phone number sends mixed signals.

Assistants also read sites other than yours when they answer, including directories, review sites and local publications. You do not control those sites, but you can make sure the information about you on them is correct and that you are listed where your customers look.

## How do you check whether ChatGPT names you?

You check by asking the questions your customers ask, in the words they use, and writing down what comes back. One try tells you very little, because answers can differ from one asking to the next. A routine tells you much more.

Here is a simple version you can do yourself:

1. Write 10 to 15 questions a real customer might ask, such as "who does kitchen remodeling near Babylon" or "how much does a roof repair cost on Long Island."
2. Ask each one in ChatGPT, Perplexity, Gemini and Claude.
3. Record whether your business is named, which competitors are named, and which sources the answer links to.
4. Repeat the same questions every month, and keep the wording the same so the months can be compared.

The sources matter as much as the names. If an answer keeps citing the same directory and you are not on it, you have found a gap.

## What does not work?

A few tactics look clever and tend to backfire, so skip them.

- **Keyword stuffing.** Repeating "best plumber Long Island" fifteen times makes a page worse for readers, and assistants are built to read language, not count phrases.
- **Fake or bought reviews.** They violate the rules of the review platforms, and a flagged profile is worse off than one with fewer reviews.
- **"Best of" lists that you wrote about yourself, presented as independent.** A list on your own site that ranks you first is advertising. Readers can tell, and honest labelling is the only version worth publishing.
- **Hiding text for crawlers.** Content that visitors cannot see does not build trust and can run against search engine guidelines.
- **Publishing once and forgetting it.** Prices, hours and services change. A page that has not been touched in years is less useful as a source.
- **Promising results.** Nobody controls what an assistant says. If an agency promises you a place in an AI answer, ask how they could possibly know.
