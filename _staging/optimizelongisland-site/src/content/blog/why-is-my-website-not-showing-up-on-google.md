If your website is not showing up on Google, it is usually one of five things:

1. **Google has not indexed the site, or cannot crawl it.** The pages are not in Google's list, so they cannot appear.
2. **The site is new and has no links or mentions.** Google may know it exists but has little reason to show it yet.
3. **The pages do not say clearly what you do and where.** Google cannot match you to a search it cannot connect you with.
4. **The search is a competitive one.** Other businesses are stronger for that phrase.
5. **For map results, the Google Business Profile is missing, unverified or incomplete.** Maps and ordinary results come from different places.

Google Search Console, a free Google product, shows which of these applies to your site. The sections below explain how to check each one yourself. If a Long Island business tells us "we're not on Google," it is almost always one of these, and often more than one.

## Is your site indexed by Google?

Indexed means Google has found your page and stored it so it can be shown in results. A page that is not indexed cannot appear for any search. This is the first thing to check.

There are two quick ways:

1. **Search for your site directly.** Type `site:yourdomain.com` into Google, with your own address. If pages from your site come back, Google has indexed at least some of them. If nothing comes back, that is a problem. Treat this as a rough check, since the count it shows is not exact.
2. **Use Search Console.** Add your site as a property and verify ownership. Open the page indexing report to see which pages are indexed and, for those that are not, the reason Google gives. The URL Inspection tool lets you paste in one page and see how Google sees it.

If your site is new, submit your sitemap in Search Console. A sitemap is a file listing the pages you want Google to know about. Many sites generate one automatically.

Google does not index every page it finds, and that is normal. What you need to know is whether your important pages, such as each service page and your contact page, are in.

## Can Google crawl your pages?

Crawling is when Google's software visits your pages to read them. If something blocks that visit, your pages cannot be indexed, however good they are. Blocks are more common than owners expect, especially after a redesign.

Check these:

- **A noindex tag.** A small instruction in a page's code that tells Google not to list it. Developers sometimes add it while a site is being built and forget to remove it at launch.
- **The robots file.** Your site has a file called robots.txt that tells crawlers which areas they may visit. One wrong line can block the whole site.
- **A "discourage search engines" setting.** Some website platforms have a checkbox for this. If it is ticked, you will not show up.
- **Pages behind a login, or built so the content does not load without special steps.** If a crawler cannot see the text, it cannot index it.
- **Server errors and broken links.** If a page returns an error, Google cannot read it.

The same robots file can also block AI crawlers, the programs that assistants such as ChatGPT use to read websites. Whether to allow them is your decision, but it should be a decision and not an accident. Our technical audit checks whether AI crawlers are allowed to read the site. For more on that side of things, see how to [show up in Google AI Overviews](/blog/how-to-show-up-in-google-ai-overviews/).

Search Console's URL Inspection tool tells you whether Google can reach a page and whether a noindex tag is stopping it.

## Do your pages say what you do and where?

Google matches searches to pages, so if your pages are vague, you will not match. A home page that says "Quality service you can trust" tells Google nothing. It could be a roofer in Babylon or a dentist in Garden City.

Look at your own pages as a stranger would:

- **Does each main service have its own page?** One page for "roof repair" and another for "roof replacement" beats one page that mentions everything.
- **Do the title and main heading name the service and the place?** For example, "Roof Repair in Suffolk County" is clear. "Welcome" is not.
- **Do you name the towns you serve?** Write them out. A service-area business that never names its towns gives Google little to go on.
- **Is your phone number, name and address the same everywhere?**
- **Do you answer real questions?** Short, direct answers to what customers ask help both Google and your visitors.

## Is your business on Google Maps?

Map results come from your Google Business Profile, not from your website. If you search for a service in your town and see three businesses on a map above the other results, those are profiles. If yours is missing, unverified or half empty, you will not be among them, even if your website is fine.

Check three things:

1. **Does your profile exist and is it verified?** Google asks you to prove the business is real, usually by phone, video or a postcard.
2. **Is it complete?** Categories, service area, hours, phone number, website link, a description and photos.
3. **Does it match your website and your listings?** The same name, address and phone number in every place.

If you work out of a home or travel to customers, you may not want to publish a street address. A service-area profile lets you list the towns you serve. Follow Google's rules for your type of business.

## Is the search too competitive for a new site?

Sometimes nothing is broken. You are simply up against businesses that have been online longer, have more reviews and are linked to from more sites. A new site searching for "plumber Long Island" is competing with every established plumber in two counties.

Be honest with yourself about that. Broad searches can take a long time, and nobody can give you a date. We will not either.

What works better for a newer business:

- **Start with specific searches.** "Emergency plumber in Bay Shore" has fewer competitors than "plumber Long Island," and the person searching it is ready to call.
- **Build one page per service and per town you serve.** Each one can match a narrower search.
- **Collect real reviews.** They help your map results and your credibility.
- **Get mentioned elsewhere.** Local directories, your trade association, community sites and local press.
- **Keep going.** Fresh, useful pages and steady improvements add up over months.

None of that is quick, and none of it is certain. It is how a site that starts out unseen becomes visible.
